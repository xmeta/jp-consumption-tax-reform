= VAT abolition compliance-productivity channel
:toc:
:stem: latexmath
:revdate: 2026-09-10

== Purpose

This module separates the institutional productivity effect of removing
VAT-specific administration from the ordinary demand effect of changing the
statutory VAT rate.

It is deliberately narrower than the repository's eventual macro-distribution
model.

The Phase-1 question is:

----
Can 0% VAT with the administrative system retained be represented as a
different policy state from complete VAT abolition, without pretending that
the resulting productivity dividend is empirically point-identified?
----

Yes.

== Policy-state separation

The regime file contains four mutually exclusive states:

[cols="3,1,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| Regime | Stylized VAT rate | VAT administration | Invoice system
| Current 8/10 | 10% / 8% | retained | retained
| Reduced 5 | 5% | retained | retained
| Zero rate | 0% | retained | retained
| Full abolition | 0% | abolished | abolished
|===

The rate vector is therefore insufficient to define the policy.

Let stem:[s_{VAT}] be the VAT administrative state and let
stem:[a(s_{VAT})] be the fraction of VAT-specific administrative obligations
removed.

For the current Phase-1 scenarios:

[stem]
++++
a(s_{VAT}) =
\begin{cases}
0,& \text{administration retained},\\
1,& \text{institutional abolition}.
\end{cases}
++++

This implies mechanically that:

----
0% VAT + administration retained != full institutional abolition
----

== Observed evidence and non-identified parameters

Observed evidence is stored separately from model assumptions.

The evidence registry establishes, among other things:

* high reported incidence of invoice-related administrative burden;
* persistent reported non-tax cost increases;
* concentration of bookkeeping responsibility in very small firms;
* METI's FY2021 survey directly reports internal tax-procedure hours for
  consumption tax in a respondent subset (n=1,514), while Q8-3 does not
  explicitly label the tax-item time measure as annual;
* a broader all-tax compliance-cost burden that is relatively larger for SMEs;
* empirical VAT-threshold bunching and evidence that compliance costs matter
  for small-enterprise responses; and
* current institutional filing, input-credit, invoice-retention, and exemption
  threshold mechanisms.

=== METI VAT-specific internal-hours bound

The corporate survey covered a nationwide target of 20,000 corporations drawn
from the Teikoku Databank business database under stated industry conditions,
and reports 4,410 valid responses (22.1%).  The consumption-tax hours item has
a smaller published n=1,514.

METI publishes nine one-decimal percentage bins from 0 hours through an
open-ended 100-hours-or-more category.  Enforcing n=1,514 and treating each
published percentage as rounded to the nearest 0.1 percentage point leaves 28
jointly feasible integer count vectors.  Using only each bin's lower endpoint
gives the conditional respondent-subset mean bound:

----
mean VAT-specific internal hours >= 15.126155878468 hours/respondent (reported Q8-3 period)
----

The 100+ category prevents any finite empirical upper bound.  If an additional
top-code cap is imposed only as a stress assumption, the corresponding upper
suprema are 24.0350 hours at a 100-hour cap, 27.4036 at 150 hours, 34.1407 at
250 hours, 50.9835 at 500 hours, and 84.6691 at 1,000 hours.  These are not
confidence limits or population upper bounds.

The published source also requires a sample-scope caution.  Results p.66 says
the hours item targets respondents answering ``yes'' to the preceding Q8-1,
whereas questionnaire Q8-3 explicitly targets Q8-1 ``No'' respondents.  The
results-page label is also numerically impossible as a literal selector: p.65
reports n=3,965 and 17.0% yes, so one-decimal rounding permits only 673--676 yes
responses, fewer than the VAT item n=1,514.  The repository preserves this
source conflict rather than inventing a sample definition.

METI p.70 also reports annual external tax-procedure outsourcing expenditure,
but it is not disaggregated by tax type.  It therefore cannot be assigned to
VAT.

Consequently, the METI result partially identifies VAT-specific *internal
hours within the observed respondent subset and reported Q8-3 period*.  Because
the tax-item hours are not explicitly labelled annual, it cannot yet be
multiplied by an annual wage benchmark as though its period were identified.
It also does not identify a Japan-wide VAT-only real-resource share of baseline
output.

=== Transition implementation cost versus persistent compliance cost

A VAT-system reform can incur *transition implementation costs* that are
conceptually distinct from the *persistent annual compliance resources* that a
full institutional abolition might release.  These quantities must not be
netted or added without an explicit time path.

SMRJ's FY2019 business-performance report gives observed program flows for the
consumption-tax reduced-rate support program.  It states that the program
subsidized part of eligible expenses for multi-rate registers, ordering-system
modifications, invoice-management-system modifications, and related
implementation.  The published counts and amounts are:

[cols="1,1,1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Scope |Grant records |Subsidy amount |Subsidy / grant record

|FY2019
|94,875
|22,914,612,620 yen
|241,524.243689 yen

|Cumulative through FY2019
|174,781
|44,659,886,587 yen
|255,519.115848 yen
|===

Because the official source says that the subsidy covers *part* of eligible
expenses, a purely accounting lower bound follows for the selected grant
records:

[stem]
++++
C^{transition,eligible}_{G} \ge S_G,
\qquad
\bar C^{transition,eligible}_{G} \ge \frac{S_G}{N_G},
++++

where stem:[S_G] is the observed subsidy flow and stem:[N_G] is the number of
grant records.  Cumulatively through FY2019, eligible transition expenditure
within these grant records is therefore at least 44.659886587 billion yen, or
255,519.115848 yen per grant record on average.

This is deliberately *not* interpreted as total private expenditure, a unique-
firm average, a population-representative cost, or a recurring annual VAT cost.
The subsidy program is selected, grant records are not proven one-to-one with
unique firms, and the source does not identify how long any software/adviser
expense persists.  It therefore supplies transition-cost context only and is
not a calibration of stem:[c_{VAT}].

Recurring VAT-specific external software/adviser expenditure remains
+NOT_IDENTIFIED+.  METI publishes annual external tax-procedure outsourcing only
for all taxes combined, while JCCI identifies invoice-related incidence of
system-modification, adviser-fee and staffing cost increases but not their yen
magnitudes.

=== Historical threshold bunching and structural compliance-cost evidence

RIETI Discussion Paper 21-E-090 supplies a separate line of evidence on the
*behavioral distortion around the VAT exemption threshold*.  The study uses a
panel of Japanese Census of Manufacture establishments and historical VAT
reforms.  Its bunching procedure estimates an upper-bound sales response for
the marginal buncher via the convergence method.  For the three 30-million-yen
threshold periods used in the structural estimation, the published inputs are:

[cols="1,1,1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Sample period |Excess bunching |Upper sales response (million yen) |stem:[\Delta y^*/y^*]

|1989--1991 |1.397 |16.498 |55.0%
|1992--1994 |1.243 |16.277 |54.3%
|1997--1999 |1.212 |16.261 |54.2%
|===

These numbers demonstrate a large *local historical* response among marginal
bunchers.  They are not an aggregate output loss: the public paper does not
supply the absolute affected-firm mass and population reweighting needed to
convert the local response into a national GDP percentage.

The same paper assumes value added stem:[v=(1-\alpha)y] and parameterizes
compliance cost as

[stem]
++++
\Theta(y;\alpha)=\theta(1-\alpha)y.
++++

With stem:[\alpha=0.65], Table 5 reports:

[cols="1,1,1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Group |Reform comparison |Tax elasticity stem:[e] |Compliance-cost stem:[\theta]

|All |1992 |0.028 |0.130
|All |1997 |0.073 |0.140
|Firms |1992 |0.391 |0.091
|Firms |1997 |0.263 |0.111
|Sole proprietors |1992 |0.054 |0.116
|Sole proprietors |1997 |0.047 |0.130
|===

For example, the paper itself illustrates that stem:[\theta=0.13] at 30 million
yen of sales and stem:[\alpha=0.65] implies 1.365 million yen under its model.
This is not an observed expenditure item.  The paper defines compliance costs
more broadly than real resource expenditure, including monetary, time,
operational, and psychological burdens.  It also states two model limitations
that are material for policy transfer: no VAT pass-through is allowed, and the
simplified tax system near the threshold is omitted.

Accordingly, this repository assigns the structural estimates the status
+MODEL_CONTINGENT_LOCAL_STRUCTURAL_COMPLIANCE_COST_PARAMETER+.  They support
the existence and potential economic importance of a threshold-allocation
channel, but they are not mapped one-for-one to either stem:[c_{VAT}] or
stem:[a_{alloc}].  The current 10-million-yen, invoice-era aggregate allocation
effect remains +NOT_IDENTIFIED_FROM_HISTORICAL_STRUCTURAL_ESTIMATES+.

=== Ten-million-yen threshold: nationalized scale and mechanism uncertainty

Ichikawa, Arudchelvan and Onji (2019) provide complementary evidence directly
at the 10-million-yen VAT exemption threshold.  Their Teikoku Databank bunching
estimates are combined with 2016 Economic Census aggregates to obtain a
nationalized scale for the distorted region.  Table 4 reports:

[cols="1,1,2",options="header"]
|===
|Quantity |Estimate |Interpretation in this repository

|Firms with sales from 8 million to below 10 million yen
|308,435
|Nationalization denominator constructed by the authors from the Economic Census

|Estimated excess-bunching firms
|56,651
|Behavioral-distortion scale; not firms proven to reduce real output

|Estimated tax windfall
|16.85 billion yen/year
|Revenue context; not welfare or GDP loss

|Lost sales: all-excess-bunchers-real-adjustment scenario
|84.98 billion yen
|Assumes every excess buncher came from the 10--11 million yen region and lost 1.5 million yen of real sales

|Lost sales: missing-mass scenario
|3.14 billion yen
|Uses observed missing mass and the same 1.5-million-yen per-firm adjustment assumption
|===

The two lost-sales figures differ by more than a factor of 27.  They are *not*
a lower and upper identification bound.  They answer different behavioral
scenario questions.  The authors explicitly warn that the all-real-adjustment
calculation can overstate the loss when bunching reflects tax avoidance, while
the missing-mass calculation is itself conditional on the assumed 1.5-million-
yen per-firm sales displacement.  Moreover, the paper notes that competing
firms may replace sales forgone by an individual buncher, so firm-level lost
sales cannot be translated one-for-one into aggregate GDP or welfare loss.

The mechanism evidence is informative but not decisive.  The paper finds that
the 8-million-yen sales bin has roughly 30% lower mean sales growth than the
11-million-yen bin, but calls the difference statistically weak.  Small missing
mass indirectly weighs against widespread real sales suppression.  At the same
time, the authors do not find direct evidence of tax avoidance and state that
the adjustment mechanism remains unresolved.  Accordingly:

----
real-adjustment share versus tax-avoidance share = NOT_IDENTIFIED
----

The study also treats the 2014 VAT increase from 5% to 8% as a quasi-
experimental opportunity.  Table 5 compares relative excess bunching before
(2004--2013) and after (2014--2018) for the same four bunching-window widths:

[cols="1,1,1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Window below threshold (million yen) |Pre stem:[b] |Post stem:[b] |Post - pre

|1.0 |0.721 |0.684 |-0.037
|1.5 |0.871 |0.712 |-0.159
|2.0 |0.900 |0.933 |0.033
|2.5 |1.104 |1.085 |-0.019
|===

For each same-window comparison the published 95% confidence intervals overlap.
Using the paper's stated decision rule, no increase in bunching is detected
after the three-percentage-point rate increase.  This is evidence against a
simple story in which the larger exemption windfall mechanically increases
bunching, and is consistent with an important compliance-burden motive.  It is
not a tax-rate elasticity estimate and does not identify the causal share of
bunching attributable to compliance cost.

This study substantially narrows the evidence gap because it uses the same
10-million-yen threshold that remains in current law.  However, its sample
predates the qualified-invoice system introduced in October 2023.  The
repository therefore records the pre-invoice 10-million-yen behavioral scale
but keeps the current invoice-era aggregate allocation effect and macro
stem:[a_{alloc}] +NOT_IDENTIFIED+.

=== Post-2023 invoice-era supplier-network adjustment

JCCI 2024 and 2025 provide direct post-introduction survey evidence on the
interaction between general-taxation businesses and exempt suppliers.  This is
the closest currently registered evidence to the invoice-network component of
stem:[a_{alloc}], but it identifies *incidence*, not a macro output effect.

For 2024, among n=782 respondents that had purchases from exempt businesses
before invoice introduction, reported post-introduction supplier status was:

[cols="2,1",options="header"]
|===
|Status |Share
|Nearly all transactions continued |74.0%
|Only some continued |7.9%
|Only very few continued |12.8%
|All transactions ended |5.2%
|===

The three explicit contraction categories sum to 25.9%.  This is a rounded
category sum, not a causal effect or a lower bound on lost transaction value.
Even the ``nearly all'' category may contain some reduction, while the survey
does not measure the value, productivity, or aggregate output consequence of
the relationships that changed.

The 2024 survey also asks n=732 respondents that still purchase from exempt
businesses about future policy.  47.1% plan to continue, while 3.8% plan to stop
all purchases, 10.7% plan to conduct purchases only in limited cases, 12.7% plan
to review when the transitional input-credit rate falls, and 2.2% plan to review
when the transition ends.  The stop-or-review categories sum to 29.4%, but remain
stated intentions rather than realized reallocation.

The 2025 survey provides a more detailed contemporaneous picture.  Among
n=1,151 registered general-taxation respondents, 43.7% currently purchase from
exempt businesses.  Among n=503 exposed respondents, 57.6% report annual exempt-
supplier purchases of at least 1 million yen.  Current responses are multiple
choice: 87.5% report maintaining prices and absorbing the burden themselves,
9.3% pass it to downstream sales prices, 5.4% lower purchase prices by all or
part of the denied credit, and 2.2% lower prices by the full consumption-tax
equivalent.  These shares must not be summed as unique-firm fractions.

For future policy, among n=502 exposed respondents, JCCI reports 42.3% planning
to review transaction prices or suppliers: 30.5% plan price review, 9.8% plan to
avoid most exempt-supplier purchases, and 2.0% plan to stop them entirely.  The
last two categories imply an 11.8% stated supplier-reduction/exit intention.
Again, this is not realized exit.

Substitution is not frictionless.  Among n=92 respondents planning to continue
with unchanged prices, multiple-response reasons include no alternative supplier
(44.6%), search effort not worth the cost (39.1%), and labor-shortage/capacity
needs requiring continued exempt-supplier transactions (38.0%).  These responses
support an economically meaningful network-friction channel, but do not measure
its welfare or productivity cost.

The surveys also show substantial registration pressure among formerly exempt
BtoB businesses.  Registration shares are 73.3% in the 2024 sample and 78.6% in
the 2025 sample; among remaining nonregistrants, 47.7% and 45.9%, respectively,
say they would consider registration if a customer requests it.  JCCI explicitly
notes that the 2024 and 2025 respondents are not the same businesses.  Therefore
the 5.3-percentage-point cross-year difference is *not* interpreted as a panel
registration transition.

The identification status is therefore upgraded from absence of current evidence
to:

----
POST_2023_NETWORK_ADJUSTMENT_INCIDENCE_OBSERVED_MACRO_EFFECT_NOT_IDENTIFIED
----

To map this evidence into stem:[a_{alloc}], the repository still needs at least
one of: transaction-value changes for adjusted relationships with a credible
counterfactual; firm-level output/productivity responses tied to supplier
registration status; population weights for the exposed network; or an explicit
structural network model identified by current data.

=== SME Agency FY2025: realized largest-customer transaction outcomes

The SME Agency FY2025 ``Invoice-System Transaction Survey'' provides a larger
official post-introduction sample and asks respondents to characterize their
*largest customer relationship*.  The survey used a Tokyo Shoko Research frame
of 50,000 businesses meeting small-business/startup criteria and obtained
15,425 responses (30.9%).  Nonresponses are excluded question by question, so
valid denominators differ.  The sample is targeted and subject to nonresponse;
its response shares are therefore not automatically population shares.

Three overall quantities materially sharpen the current invoice-era evidence:

[cols="2,1,1,2",options="header"]
|===
|Question |Published share |Rounded-count-compatible responses |Interpretation

|Customer required invoice registration as a condition for continued transactions (n=15,321)
|14.3%
|2,184--2,198
|Observed registration-pressure incidence

|Largest-customer transaction price reduced *or transaction stopped* after invoice introduction (n=14,732)
|3.8%
|553--567
|Realized adverse transaction-outcome incidence

|No invoice-related price-negotiation opportunity was provided (n=14,690)
|12.8%
|1,873--1,887
|Observed negotiation-access friction
|===

The integer ranges invert the report's one-decimal published percentages under
the recorded denominators.  They are not confidence intervals; they only show
which integer response counts are compatible with the rounded shares.

The 3.8% outcome is stronger evidence than a stated future intention, but it is
not yet a macro allocation-loss estimate.  The questionnaire combines price
reduction and full transaction stop in one category, does not report the yen
value of the affected relationship, and does not identify what would have
happened absent the invoice system.  Even a stopped relationship need not imply
one-for-one aggregate output loss if customers substitute toward another
supplier.

Industry heterogeneity is substantial within the survey.  The largest-customer
price-reduction/stop shares are 4.6% in manufacturing, 5.9% in construction,
7.9% in transport/postal, 6.4% in wholesale, 3.4% in retail, 3.3% in services,
and 3.2% in the residual group.  Customer registration requirements also range
widely, from 8.8% in the residual group to 32.7% in construction.  These values
identify heterogeneity inside the targeted respondent sample, not population-
weighted causal effects.

The final report reproduces a 3.6% price-reduction/transaction-stop share for
the December 2023 survey (n=3,674) and 3.8% for July 2025 (n=14,732).  The
survey frame expanded and the waves are not a panel; therefore the 0.2-point
difference is explicitly *not* interpreted as a causal change over time.

Together with the JCCI evidence, the current identification status becomes:

----
POST_2023_REALIZED_ADVERSE_TRANSACTION_INCIDENCE_OBSERVED_MACRO_EFFECT_NOT_IDENTIFIED
----

A defensible mapping into stem:[a_{alloc}] still requires transaction-value
magnitudes and either a credible counterfactual or an identified structural
model, plus population weights and treatment of substitution/reallocation.

=== BSWS wage-conversion bridge

RIETI 21-P-018 states that tax-procedure labor time was valued using
industry-specific hourly wages from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare
Basic Survey on Wage Structure.  The paper does not identify the exact e-Stat
table or the exact arithmetic used to construct its hourly wage, so this
repository does not claim an exact replication of that unpublished step.

For 2019 e-Stat Table 1 (statInfId 000031919751; DB sid 0003084009), the
repository preserves four official components for 17 industry-total/major-
industry rows: scheduled actual hours, overtime actual hours, regular cash
earnings, and scheduled cash earnings.  Two transparent wage candidates are
then defined:

[stem]
++++
w^{sched}_j = rac{1000\,S_j}{H^{sched}_j},
\qquad
w^{eff}_j = rac{1000\,R_j}{H^{sched}_j+H^{ot}_j},
++++

where stem:[S_j] is scheduled cash earnings in thousand yen, stem:[R_j] is
regular cash earnings in thousand yen, and the two hour variables are monthly
actual hours.  For the industry-total row these yield 1,923.125 yen/hour and
1,953.757225433526 yen/hour.  Across the 16 major-industry rows the transparent
rate range is approximately 1,466--2,852 yen/hour depending on formula and
industry.

Combining either hourly rate with the METI conditional lower-bound hours is a
*mechanical cross-source conversion*, not a newly identified annual cost.  At
the industry-total rates:

----
15.126155878468 h x 1,923.125 yen/h = about 29,089 yen/respondent/Q8-3 reported period
15.126155878468 h x 1,953.757225... yen/h = about 29,553 yen/respondent/Q8-3 reported period
----

The full 34-scenario industry/formula table is retained for sensitivity.  The
conversion still lacks an explicitly annual Q8-3 period, respondent-subset
industry weights, population-selection correction, temporal alignment between
2019 wages and the FY2021 survey, and VAT-specific external outsourcing.
Therefore it cannot be interpreted as Japan-wide stem:[c_{VAT}].

Therefore:

----
c_VAT  = NOT_IDENTIFIED
rho    = NOT_IDENTIFIED
a_alloc= NOT_IDENTIFIED
----

where:

* stem:[c_{VAT}] is the counterfactual real-resource share tied specifically to
  VAT administration;
* stem:[\rho] is the productive redeployment fraction; and
* stem:[a_{alloc}] is the abolition-only allocative-efficiency level effect.

The numerical grids are stress tests, not confidence intervals, identified
sets, estimates, or empirical bounds.
== Compliance-productivity channel

For a regime with administrative-removal fraction stem:[m], define released
VAT administrative resources as:

[stem]
++++
R_{VAT}=m c_{VAT}.
++++

Only a fraction stem:[\rho] is assumed to become measured productive output:

[stem]
++++
\Delta y_{compliance}=\rho R_{VAT}
=m\rho c_{VAT}.
++++

This explicitly prevents the forbidden one-for-one rule:

[stem]
++++
\Delta y_{compliance}=c_{VAT}
++++

unless a stress-test point *separately* sets both stem:[m=1] and
stem:[\rho=1].

The allocative channel is kept separate:

[stem]
++++
\Delta y_{allocation}=m a_{alloc}.
++++

The institutional output-level sensitivity is therefore:

[stem]
++++
\Delta y_{institution}
=m(\rho c_{VAT}+a_{alloc}).
++++

The module does not use the VAT rate in this equation.

That is intentional: rate effects belong to the demand/tax wedge channel, not
to the abolition-of-administration channel.

== Transition-growth reporting

If a level effect is mechanically spread over stem:[T] years, the module
reports the annualized transition contribution:

[stem]
++++
g_{institution,T}
=
(1+\Delta y_{institution})^{1/T}-1.
++++

This is *not* a forecast of annual real GDP growth.

It is only the annualized contribution of the model-contingent level effect.
The repository objective stem:[g_t>0] still requires the full macro model.

== End-to-end Phase-1 result

All three administration-retained regimes satisfy, for every stress point:

[stem]
++++
\Delta y_{institution}=0.
++++

This includes the 0% VAT regime.

The full-abolition regime spans:

----
minimum institutional level effect = 0
maximum institutional level effect = 0.006
----

The 0.006 value is the maximum of the chosen stress grid:

[stem]
++++
1\times(1\times0.005+0.001)=0.006.
++++

It is not an empirical upper bound.

The result is useful because it proves the architecture:

----
rate reduction channel     : separate
administration abolition   : separate
compliance resource release: separate
allocative efficiency      : separate
financing                  : separate
distribution / FGT2        : not yet linked
----

== Phase-2 requirements

Issue #26 remains open after this Phase-1 implementation.

Before policy optimization, additional work is required to identify or bound:

* a public numeric VAT-hours source with an identified reporting period and population weights, or access to the historical METI microdata needed to estimate it;
* VAT-specific monetary resource costs by firm size and industry;
* the exact RIETI BSWS hourly-wage construction, or a justified replacement;
* the fraction of saved owner/worker time that becomes productive activity;
* recurring VAT-specific software/adviser costs and their duration; transition implementation cost is now partially bounded only for selected subsidy grant records;
* identification of the real-adjustment versus tax-avoidance mixture needed to translate 10-million-yen bunching into real resource/output effects;
* transaction-value magnitudes and a credible counterfactual/structural mapping for the now-observed post-2023 realized adverse transaction and network-adjustment incidence, with population weights and substitution treatment, before any macro +a_alloc+ bound;
* household incidence by income/wealth/age group;
* demand and pass-through effects;
* revenue and financing effects under JGB, income-tax, asset-tax and mixed
  packages; and
* inflation, debt/GDP, interest-rate and JGB-market feedbacks.

Only after those links exist can the repository jointly report GDP, growth,
Gini, FGT2, fiscal and debt outcomes for Issue #26.
